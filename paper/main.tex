\begin{abstract}
Turkish is agglutinative: a single surface word may carry a root and several
suffixes, and slot values are often embedded inside that morphological
structure. Because slot filling is a token-level sequence-labelling task whose
labels depend on token boundaries, the segmentation choice should matter most
precisely for slot filling --- yet this intersection has not been studied
systematically. We present a controlled comparison of segmentation strategies
(model-native subword, whitespace, unsupervised morphological, and character) as
an independent variable for \emph{joint} intent detection and slot filling in
Turkish, on the open MASSIVE-tr benchmark, across a monolingual encoder (BERTurk)
and two multilingual encoders (mBERT, XLM-R). Our contributions are
(i)~a fair cross-scheme BIO label re-alignment procedure with a mandatory
round-trip validation that we verify recovers gold word labels exactly for all
encoders and schemes; (ii)~the controlled segmentation comparison itself; and
(iii)~a fine-grained, morphology-aware slot error analysis along seven axes that
isolates \emph{where} and \emph{why} morphology affects slot filling. A
training-free tokenizer analysis already shows that the multilingual mBERT
fragments Turkish most and aligns with morphology least, predicting before any
training that it should gain most from morphological pre-segmentation.

\section{Introduction}
Task-oriented dialogue understanding couples two tasks. \emph{Intent detection}
is sentence-level classification --- mapping an utterance to a user goal such as
\textsc{alarm\_set} or \textsc{weather\_query}. \emph{Slot filling} is
token-level sequence labelling: each token is tagged in the BIO scheme to mark
the spans that carry slot values (a date, a city, a person). The two are usually
learned jointly, since the intent constrains which slots are plausible and vice
versa~\cite{chen2019bert}. Critically, a slot label is attached to a
\emph{token boundary}: which spans exist, and where they begin and end, is
determined by how the input is tokenized.

This makes slot filling acutely sensitive to segmentation in an agglutinative
language. In Turkish, the orthographic word boundary and the meaning-unit
boundary do not coincide: a single surface word packs a root and a sequence of
suffixes, and the slot value is frequently embedded \emph{inside} that
structure. The word \emph{faturalarımdan} (``from my bills'') decomposes as
\emph{fatura}\,+\,\emph{lar}\,+\,\emph{ım}\,+\,\emph{dan}
(bill\,+\,\textsc{plural}\,+\,\textsc{p1sg}\,+\,\textsc{ablative}); the lexical
content a slot tagger must recover sits in the root and the early morphemes,
wrapped in inflection. The segmentation decision therefore determines where a
slot label can fall, which makes slot filling the natural locus at which to ask
whether segmentation matters.

Standard transformer tokenizers (WordPiece, BPE, SentencePiece) are
frequency-driven and learned without morphological supervision; they routinely
place subword boundaries that violate morpheme boundaries. We confirm this
quantitatively (Section~\ref{sec:tok}): on MASSIVE-tr slot words, the
multilingual mBERT produces $2.67$ subwords per word and respects morpheme
boundaries in only $16\%$ of fragmented words, against $2.05$ and $41\%$ for the
monolingual BERTurk. The central question follows: does a morphology-aware
pre-segmentation yield a measurable gain for Turkish slot filling --- and for
which words and which encoders --- or is the pretrained model's own subwording
already sufficient?

Two literatures bear on this question but leave its intersection open. Studies of
tokenization in Turkish transformers measure intrinsic or downstream effects on
tasks such as language modelling, NLI, NER and parsing, but not task-oriented
NLU. Conversely, Turkish intent$+$slot studies adopt a single, fixed tokenizer
and never treat segmentation as a variable. Neither addresses the methodological
crux that any such comparison must solve: BIO labels defined over surface words
must be \emph{re-projected} onto each scheme's units and then folded back to a
common reference unit, or the F1 scores are not comparable. We scope our claims
narrowly to slot filling and task-oriented NLU; we make no general
``Turkish tokenization'' claim.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The first systematic comparison that treats segmentation as a
  \emph{controlled independent variable} for Turkish joint intent$+$slot filling
  on MASSIVE-tr, across one monolingual and two multilingual encoders.
  \item A fair cross-scheme \emph{BIO label re-alignment} procedure with a
  mandatory round-trip validation; we report that it recovers gold word labels
  exactly ($100\%$) for every encoder and scheme, and we argue this guard is a
  reusable methodological contribution and a pitfall warning.
  \item A fine-grained slot error analysis along seven axes --- affix count,
  surface rarity, model fragmentation, morpheme-boundary violation, root$|$suffix
  boundary, domain, and slot type.
  \item A full reproducibility package: code, segmentation scripts, the alignment
  validation, run configurations, and the seed list.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{Turkish intent detection and slot filling.}
Joint models in the style of Joint~BERT~\cite{chen2019bert} have been applied to
Turkish by translating and adapting the ATIS benchmark and reporting intent
accuracy and slot F1 with a fixed, standard tokenizer~\cite{buyuk2023,
turkishatis2020}. These studies establish baselines but do not vary the
tokenizer; segmentation is held constant and its effect on slot filling is never
isolated.

\paragraph{Tokenization and segmentation for Turkish.}
A complementary line studies tokenization itself. Toraman et
al.~\cite{toraman2022} analyse the impact of tokenizer choice on Turkish language
models; Altınok~\cite{altinok2026} evaluates Turkish subword strategies at scale,
jointly varying vocabulary and corpus size with a morphology-aware diagnostic
toolkit (boundary-level F1 against gold morphemes, over-/under-segmentation
indices); and hybrid morphology-aware tokenizers have been proposed for
Turkish~\cite{tokenswithmeaning2025}. The evaluation toolkit for morphologically
rich languages now includes intrinsic metrics such as fertility and boundary
alignment against morpheme references~\cite{tokalign70}. This literature, however, targets language
modelling and tasks such as NLI, STS, NER, POS and dependency parsing --- not
task-oriented NLU, and not slot filling, where the token boundary \emph{is} the
label boundary. Morphological segmentation has also improved Turkish machine
translation, and morphology-aware segmentation has been studied for related
agglutinative languages, motivating its examination here.

\paragraph{Gap.}
The intersection is open: a controlled segmentation comparison for Turkish
\emph{joint intent$+$slot filling}, with a fair cross-scheme BIO alignment
methodology and a morpheme-boundary-level slot error analysis. Tokenization
studies do not cover slot filling; slot-filling studies do not vary segmentation.
We deliberately position our contribution at this intersection and at the
alignment methodology, rather than as a general claim about Turkish tokenization,
for which the recent literature is already strong.

\section{Data}
We use \textbf{MASSIVE-tr}~\cite{fitzgerald2022massive}, the Turkish split of
Amazon MASSIVE (CC~BY~4.0): $11{,}514$ train / $2{,}033$ dev / $2{,}974$ test
utterances over $18$ domains, with \textbf{60 intents} and \textbf{55 slot
types} ($109$ BIO labels) and official splits, ensuring comparability with prior
and future work. Utterances average $5.5$ tokens. We parse the bracketed
\texttt{annot\_utt} annotations into word-level BIO tags deterministically. Of
the $17{,}668$ slot-bearing words in the training split, \textbf{18\% carry at
least one affix} (Morfessor estimate). This figure frames our expectations: the
agglutinative pressure the study targets is concentrated in a minority of slot
words, so an overall F1 gap from morphological pre-segmentation may be modest
even where the effect on affixed words is large --- which is precisely the
question the error analysis (Section~\ref{sec:error}) is designed to answer.
As an optional second domain, our code also loads MultiATIS++-tr (flight
domain)~\cite{xu2020multiatis} in the JointBERT
\texttt{seq.in}/\texttt{seq.out}/\texttt{label} format.

\section{Method}
\subsection{Segmentation strategies (independent variable)}
Re-training a pretrained model's embedding table from scratch under each scheme
is infeasible in our setting; we therefore adopt a \emph{pre-segmentation}
paradigm: the text is first split by a strategy, and the resulting pre-tokens are
then sub-tokenized by the model's own tokenizer. We compare four strategies.
\textbf{Native} feeds the raw string to the tokenizer (the current practice and
our control), with labels aligned by character offsets. \textbf{Whitespace} feeds
one pre-token per surface word. \textbf{Morphological} splits each word into
morphemes with an unsupervised Morfessor~\cite{virpioja2013morfessor} model
trained only on the training-split word types (a rule-based Zeyrek backend, a
Python port of Zemberek~\cite{akin2007zemberek}, is also provided).
\textbf{Character} splits each word into characters, a lower-bound reference.

We verified empirically that \emph{native and whitespace produce identical
tokenization} for all three encoders on MASSIVE-tr ($0\%$ divergence over
$11{,}514$ utterances), including the SentencePiece-based XLM-R, whose
space-prefix marker preserves word boundaries on whitespace-separated input. The
meaningful contrast is therefore model-default subword (native\,$\approx$\,
whitespace) vs.\ morphological vs.\ character; we retain both default variants as
a reported control. The unsupervised Morfessor backend genuinely segments Turkish
(it splits $\sim$60\% of word types, e.g.\ \emph{faturalarımdan}\,$\rightarrow$\,
\emph{fatura}+\emph{ları}+\emph{mdan}); morphological ambiguity is resolved by the
single most-probable analysis, which we treat as a known error source. We use
Morfessor as the primary morphological backend rather than the rule-based Zeyrek
because, on MASSIVE-tr slot words, Zeyrek is highly conservative --- it segments
only $3.7\%$ of slot word types (many are proper nouns and place names with
phonological alternations it does not analyse) --- whereas Morfessor offers far
higher coverage and surface-faithful splits. The two backends agree exactly on
$48.7\%$ of slot word types; where Zeyrek does segment, Morfessor matches its
boundaries with precision $0.73$. We report this divergence transparently as a
limitation of the unsupervised proxy.

\subsection{Tokenizer morphological alignment (motivation)}
\label{sec:tok}
Before any training we quantify how each encoder's native tokenizer relates to
Turkish morphology, using fertility (subwords per word) and the boundary
precision/recall/F1 of subword boundaries against morpheme boundaries from an
unsupervised reference (Morfessor), following tokenizer-evaluation practice for
morphologically rich languages~\cite{tokalign70,toraman2022}. Boundary metrics
are computed over the words the reference segments; ``respect'' is the fraction
of multi-subword words whose cuts never fall inside a morpheme.
Table~\ref{tab:tok} shows that the multilingual mBERT fragments Turkish most
(highest fertility) and aligns with morphology least (lowest boundary F1 and
respect), whereas the monolingual BERTurk is the most morphologically coherent.
This predicts, before training, that mBERT should gain most from morphological
pre-segmentation (H3).

\subsection{Cross-scheme BIO re-alignment}
The independent variable changes the token units, so word-level BIO tags must be
re-projected onto each scheme's pre-tokens. Splitting a word labelled $B$-$x$ into
$k$ pieces yields $B$-$x,\,I$-$x,\,\dots$ ($k{-}1$ copies of $I$-$x$); an $I$-$x$
word yields $I$-$x\cdots$; an $O$ word yields $O\cdots$. This preserves the span
semantics exactly: one $B$ per span and no spurious internal boundaries. Each
pre-token is then sub-tokenized; the first sub-token of each pre-token carries the
label and the rest are masked in the loss. Crucially, \emph{evaluation always
folds predictions back to the surface word}, by reading the prediction at each
word's \emph{head sub-token} (the first sub-token of its first pre-token), so that
F1 is computed at a common reference unit and is comparable across schemes.

Because mis-alignment silently inflates or deflates F1, we make correctness a hard
precondition: projecting gold tags through a scheme and folding them back must
recover the original word labels exactly. Table~\ref{tab:align} reports this
round-trip on dev for every encoder and scheme; all reach $100\%$ with zero
mismatches, and only character segmentation loses a small number of words to the
length cap. Any scheme failing this guard is excluded from experiments.

\subsection{Joint model and ablations}
A shared encoder feeds two heads: an intent classifier off the \texttt{[CLS]}
state and a token slot tagger off the sequence states; the loss is
$\mathcal{L}_{\text{intent}}+\lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{slot}}$~\cite{chen2019bert}.
We consider four ablations that interact with segmentation: \emph{subword
pooling}, which forms a fragmented word's representation from the first, mean, or
max of its sub-tokens (directly probing how fragmentation should be aggregated);
a linear-chain CRF over the supervised head positions; a focal slot loss to
counter the dominance of the $O$ class; and constrained (BIO-repair) decoding
that removes invalid $I$-transitions at inference.

\section{Experimental Setup}
Encoders: BERTurk~\cite{schweter2020berturk}
(\texttt{dbmdz/bert-base-turkish-cased}), mBERT~\cite{devlin2019bert}
(\texttt{bert-base-multilingual-cased}), and XLM-R~\cite{conneau2020xlmr}
(\texttt{xlm-roberta-base}).
The primary matrix crosses $3$ encoders $\times$ $3$ segmentations (native,
morphological, character) $\times$ $3$ seeds $\{42,123,2024\}$; whitespace is
omitted from the default sweep because it is identical to native here.
Optimisation uses AdamW (lr $5\!\times\!10^{-5}$, batch $32$, $5$ epochs, linear
warmup $0.1$, \texttt{max\_length}$=64$), with model selection on dev frame
accuracy. Metrics are intent accuracy, span-level slot F1 (seqeval, strict IOB2),
and \emph{frame accuracy} (intent and all slots correct), the strictest measure.
We report mean$\pm$std over seeds and test paired differences with both a
bootstrap on frame accuracy and an exact McNemar test. As sanity checks we
reference published MASSIVE-tr baselines.

\section{Error Analysis}
\label{sec:error}
The headline contribution beyond the comparison is to explain \emph{where} and
\emph{why} morphology affects slot filling. We stratify per-word slot errors along
seven axes: (i)~affix count (Morfessor morpheme-count proxy), (ii)~surface-form
training frequency, (iii)~model fragmentation (sub-tokens per word),
(iv)~segmentation shift --- whether the model's subword boundaries violate
morpheme boundaries, (v)~the root$|$suffix boundary respected vs.\ violated,
(vi)~domain/scenario, and (vii)~slot type.

Preliminary single-encoder diagnostics already show the predicted directions.
Error rate rises monotonically with affix count and with surface rarity:
affix-heavy and rare surface forms are markedly harder, consistent with the view
that inflection obscures the lexical content a slot tagger must recover. Errors
also concentrate where the model's subwording violates the root$|$suffix boundary
relative to where that boundary is respected --- a direct, boundary-level signal
that getting the root split right matters. We will report the full per-encoder,
per-scheme tables, which quantify the affix-heavy subset where morphological
pre-segmentation is expected to help most (H2).

\section{Conclusion and Limitations}
We framed segmentation as a controlled variable for Turkish joint intent and slot
filling, contributed a validated cross-scheme BIO re-alignment procedure, and a
seven-axis morphology-aware error analysis, supported by a training-free tokenizer
analysis that motivates the study and predicts which encoders should benefit most
from morphological pre-segmentation. Limitations: a single primary benchmark and
domain (MASSIVE-tr; MultiATIS++-tr is supported but optional); an unsupervised
morphological proxy (Morfessor) rather than a full rule-based analyzer for the
main strategy; and morphological ambiguity resolved by the most-probable analysis.
These are explicit error sources rather than confounds, and the reproducibility
package allows each to be revisited.
